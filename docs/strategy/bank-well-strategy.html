Bank Well · strategy for the yearly figure

How Bank Well can produce a yearly church-banking figure

A plan for turning the hackathon work into a number Bank Well can publish every year, run by a small team on a budget of about £50 a year.

Prepared 28 October 2026 from the handover notes, the follow-up call and a round of source research. Sources were checked on 28 October 2026. Points marked “not yet verified” still need checking.

Summary

  • Build a yearly data process, not an app. It runs once a year from public registers and published accounts, and produces two numbers: the share of churches banking with banks rated 5 by bank.green, and the cash churches hold at banks not rated 5.
  • Start from the charity registers. Count the registered churches across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland well, then estimate the churches that are not registered rather than trying to find each one.
  • Publish a range and the method. For example “around 30% (27–34%)”, with a plain explanation of what was counted and where the line was drawn.
  • “Current accounts” cannot be measured from public data. The honest measure is cash at bank and in hand from published accounts, which is an upper bound on current-account balances.
  • Cost is not the problem. Reading the accounts costs roughly £3 a year with the cheaper approach described below. The hard parts are the definitions, the coverage of unregistered churches and keeping the process running.

What we can and cannot measure

The hackathon verified the bank for 3,857 churches out of about 31,700 on the Charity Commission register. About 31% of them bank with a bank rated 5. The corrected figure for cash at the worst-rated banks is £180.4m (the deck still shows the earlier £178.7m).

Current accounts versus cash at bank

The call asked for money held in current accounts. Public data cannot give that:

  • Charity accounting rules group cash by purpose, not by account type. “Cash at bank and in hand” mixes current accounts with deposits that can be withdrawn on demand.
  • Smaller churches (income up to £250,000) report bank balances per fund, not per account.
  • The Charity Commission’s own bulk data has one cash field, which includes “deposits … repayable on demand”, and only charities with income over £500,000 file it.
  • The “Bankers” line in accounts is probably optional, so it is not a complete list of where a church’s money sits (not yet verified).

Money from the Parish Giving Scheme is paid into the church’s own bank account, so it is already inside the cash figure. Money held for a parish by its diocese is not, and how it is reported is not yet verified.

Proposed wording: “cash at bank and in hand, as reported in published accounts. This is an upper bound on current-account balances.”

The approach: three tiers

Each tier builds on the one before. Tier 1 alone gives a publishable figure.

Tier 1 · year one

Registered churches across the UK

  • Who counts: churches on the Charity Commission register (England and Wales); Scottish churches from the OSCR register, which has a “Designated Religious body” yes/no field; Northern Irish churches from the CCNI register; and umbrella bodies such as Catholic diocesan trusts and Church of England diocesan boards.
  • Which bank: the “Bankers” line in the accounts, plus sort codes published on church websites. Both methods already exist from the hackathon.
  • How much cash: read from the accounts PDFs. Simple scripts read the clear documents; a low-cost AI model reads only the pages the scripts cannot.
  • Fix the gap in the sample: the Charity Commission document store went down during the hackathon, so there are no accounts for churches with income between £150,000 and £500,000. Re-downloading the full register fixes this.
  • Result: the two numbers, scaled up from the verified churches by denomination and income band, published as a range.
Tier 2 · closing the gap

Churches that are not on a register

Most Church of England parishes with income under £100,000 do not have to register, so they are missing from Tier 1. To account for them, work backwards from buildings:

  • Start from a list of buildings. OpenStreetMap and Ordnance Survey’s free OS Open Map Local both mark places of worship. The hackathon already collected 44,515 church buildings from OpenStreetMap.
  • Match each building to a register entry by postcode and name. The hackathon’s matching script already links 3,533 buildings directly, plus 192 through an umbrella body.
  • What is left over is the unregistered churches. Counting them by denomination gives the full picture of how many churches exist. Their banking is then estimated from registered churches of the same denomination, rather than found one by one.
  • The biggest single gain: the Church of England’s A Church Near You site lists every parish with its legal name and website. With written permission, parish websites can be checked for sort codes, covering many of its roughly 12,500 parishes.
Tier 3 · campaign outputs

Who to approach first

  • A ranking of umbrella bodies (dioceses, trusts, church networks) holding the most cash at banks not rated 5. This is useful to groups such as Christian Climate Action. Check the OSCR licence terms before naming anyone from Scottish data.
  • A short survey for the largest churches whose bank is still unknown, with follow-up for the most important ones.

How it runs

  • Data lives in a single database file on a shared server Bank Well can reach, not in a personal Google Sheet (which hit API limits during the hackathon).
  • It runs once a year, in November. Most churches’ financial year ends on 31 December and accounts are due within 10 months (deadline not yet verified).
  • A written runbook lets any of the team run it, with a hard spending cap per run.

What we are not doing, and why

IdeaWhy not, for now
Building an appThe figure updates once a year. An app adds hosting, logins and maintenance for something a scheduled process does.
Searching every UK addressAn address does not say whether it is a church. Only Ordnance Survey’s AddressBase marks places of worship (code ZW), and it is paid or limited to public-sector bodies. The free building lists above give the same starting point.
Copying A Church Near You without permissionIts terms say not to “reproduce, duplicate, copy or re-sell any part of our site”. Ask the Church of England instead.
Using the existing UK address fileOnly once the licence it came under is known. Publishing anything built from AddressBase without a licence is a risk.
Running it quarterlyAccounts are published once a year, so a quarterly run has no new data.
The Methodist find-a-church searchThe site blocks automated use of that search.

Decisions for the team

Each decision below has a recommended answer. Whatever is chosen for year one becomes the basis of every later year, so changing it later breaks the comparison between years. Decisions 8 to 10 came out of reviewing this strategy and are marked in amber.

1. What counts as a church?

Recommended: registered charities with a religion purpose, plus umbrella bodies. Unregistered churches are estimated, not counted one by one.

2. Which money do we measure?

Recommended: cash at bank and in hand, described as an upper bound. State clearly that current accounts cannot be measured.

3. How precise should the figure be?

Recommended: a range, such as “around 30% (27–34%)”, rather than a single exact number.

4. How often?

Recommended: yearly, run in November.

5. Who runs it, and what is the spending cap?

Recommended: one named person each year, following the runbook, with a hard cap of £10 per run.

6. What goes into year one?

Recommended: Tier 1, plus the request to the Church of England. Tier 2’s building matching if time allows.

7. Can we name organisations publicly?

Recommended: name umbrella bodies only, never individual churches.

8. How do we treat church deposit funds?

Many churches keep cash in church investment funds, such as the CBF Church of England Deposit Fund, rather than at a bank. bank.green does not rate these. Are they ethical, unrated, or left out? This could move a large share of the total.

9. What is “one church”?

One charity can run several buildings, and a diocese is a single charity. Do we count charities, congregations or buildings?

10. Do we also track the same churches each year?

Recommended: yes, alongside the headline figure, so switching can be measured separately from changes in who was sampled.

What this strategy does not yet account for

These are open problems found when reviewing the plan. Some may change the number; others may stop it being useful or stop it happening. They are listed most serious first within each group.

Could make the number wrong

HighChurch deposit funds

Cash held in church investment funds rather than banks is not rated by bank.green and is not yet covered by any decision. See decision 8.

HighCounting the same money twice

A diocesan trust’s accounts may include money that its parishes also report. Adding umbrella bodies on top of parishes could double-count.

HighWhat “one church” means

The percentage changes depending on whether we count charities, congregations or buildings. See decision 9.

MediumBank brands and changing ratings

NatWest, RBS and Coutts belong to one group, and RBS is currently excluded as unrated. bank.green ratings can also change between years, which would move the figure even if no church switched. Fix the ratings used for each year.

MediumUnregistered churches are different

They are smaller. Applying registered churches’ banking patterns to them assumes small churches bank like large ones, and there is no income data to correct for it.

MediumWhich churches show up

Churches with websites showing sort codes, or accounts naming their bankers, are not typical. The sort code on a website is usually the giving account, not necessarily where reserves are held.

Could make the number less useful

HighShowing whether campaigns work

Accounts are one to two years behind, so a switch made this year shows two years later. The figure can also move because a different set of churches was sampled. Tracking the same churches each year (decision 10) addresses this.

MediumStanding up to challenge

A bank, a diocese or a journalist may question the method. Have a statistician review it before the first figures are published (Sam was suggested on the call), and write the method down before the results come in.

Could stop it happening

HighDepending on one person

The method currently relies on Ben and on AI-assisted tools. Stefan is not staying long term. If the person who can run it is unavailable in November, there is no figure that year. Decide whether a runbook is enough or whether someone needs training.

MediumAsking the Church of England for data

Bank Well would be asking for help while campaigning about church banking. The request may be refused or may affect the relationship, so its framing needs care.

MediumData protection

The accounts contain trustee names. Bank Well probably needs a short written record of its lawful basis for processing them, especially before sharing anything with partners (not yet verified).

MediumOpenStreetMap licence

OpenStreetMap’s licence is share-alike, so publishing a church list built from it may require releasing that list under the same terms (licence wording not yet checked).

A question for the team

OpenIs one headline figure the most useful thing?

A breakdown by diocese or church network, or a “how to switch” pack sent to churches already identified, might change more behaviour than a single percentage. This is a campaign choice for Bank Well.

Evidence and sources

Checked on 28 October 2026.

  • Scottish register: free daily download under the Open Government Licence, with conditions against direct marketing and against presenting a list as the official register. Fields include “Designated Religious body”, purposes, postcode, income and website. OSCR download page
  • Northern Irish register: a CSV export from the register search, listed as Open Government Licence on data catalogues. Columns not yet verified. OpenDataNI
  • Charity Commission bulk data: the cash field covers “deposits … repayable on demand”, and only charities with income over £500,000 file it. Full register download
  • A Church Near You: one entry per Church of England church, with no charity number or finance data; terms bar copying. Terms
  • AddressBase: code ZW marks places of worship; paid or public-sector only; being retired in autumn 2027. Ordnance Survey
  • AI pricing: Claude Haiku 5.5 at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 output, half price for batch jobs. Anthropic pricing
  • Small-church accounts: bank balances reported per fund. Church of England guidance
Privacy: the downloaded accounts contain trustee names. They stay on the data server, are never put in a shared code repository, and only totals are ever published.
